### Core concepts and findings

- The address distinguishes relational clientelism, which involves continuing asymmetric exchange, from electoral clientelism, which concentrates material inducements around campaigns.
- Its broad working definition of money politics covers last-minute attempts to influence votes through cash, goods, or other material compensation.
- Survey evidence for the 2014 and 2019 legislative elections estimates that roughly one-quarter to one-third of voters were exposed, with the highest measure at 33.1% in both post-election surveys.
- The 2014 calculation equates the highest estimate to roughly 62 million of 187 million registered voters.
- Reported inducements were predominantly cash, followed by staple goods and household items.
- In the comparison presented, Indonesia's 33% rate was roughly twice the 14.22% cross-country average and ranked third among the countries in the table.
- The address argues that the practice has become normalized socially and rhetorically despite being illegal.
- Standard modernization indicators such as individual income, education, and rural residence were not reliable predictors in the presented models.
- Partisanship, political interest, and low political efficacy were important predictors in the multivariate discussion. Support for democracy alone did not reliably predict rejection of money politics.
- Only about 15% of voters in the 2014 survey identified closely with a party, while about 85% were non-partisan/swing voters. Exposure was proportionally higher among partisans, but the much larger swing-voter population meant more recipients in absolute numbers were non-partisan.
- The proposed explanation is a **personal-loyalist strategy**: candidates intend to reach a loyal base but rely on personal, kinship, religious, ethnic, patronage, and broker networks, which causes mistargeting.
- Agency loss among campaign brokers includes working for multiple candidates, taking a portion of distribution money, exaggerating influence, and supplying unreliable voter lists.
- Exposure was associated with higher turnout in the presented comparison: 81% among offered voters versus 74% among those not exposed.
- About 10.2% of exposed voters reported that the inducement affected their choice. The address argues that this seemingly modest effect can decide candidate-level contests because the average internal winning margin discussed was only about 1.65%.
- The prisoner’s-dilemma mechanism explains continued spending: candidates fear unilateral restraint will cause defeat when rivals distribute money. In the broker survey, 92% reported competing against other teams also distributing inducements.
- The institutional explanation emphasizes weak party identification, candidate-centered competition, open-list proportional representation, personal-vote incentives, and informal broker networks.

### Recommendations made in the address

- Review the open-list proportional system and consider a more party-centered/closed-list design, while acknowledging risks of elite oligarchy and weak accountability.
- Pair a closed-list option with internal primary elections or conventions to reduce elite capture.
- If open lists remain, reduce district magnitude to reduce incentives for narrow personal-vote competition.
- Consider the accountability benefits and pluralism costs of single-member district alternatives.
- Strengthen enforcement and deterrent sanctions.
- Coordinate voter education among election authorities, media, and civil society so voters understand the risk of reversed accountability after accepting inducements.
